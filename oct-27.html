What happened at the Oct 27 Board meeting

In short

  • Superintendent Michael LaSusa recommended ending dual language immersion at CP and replacing it with a "Spanish-intensive" program: one 45-minute Spanish class every day, with math and science taught in English.
  • CP would also become a neighborhood-only school, with no opting in from other parts of town.
  • Nothing has been decided yet. The Board could vote at its next meeting. The superintendent wants a decision by Thanksgiving, before kindergarten registration.

The superintendent called this "four times as much" as anyone else in the district. Critics noted it is still less than half the Spanish CP students get today.

2. Why the superintendent is recommending it

The full presentation starts at 0:10:41 and runs about 75 minutes. The main reasons given:

Note: the data behind these reasons has not been shared with parents, despite pending public-records (OPRA) requests, so families have not been able to check it themselves.

  1. The current program as practiced is not technically "Dual Language Immersion".
    • CP is a "one-way" program (mostly English speakers learning Spanish). It has never had enough Spanish-dominant students to be "two-way".
    • By the superintendent's count, actual Spanish time falls short of half even in core subjects. Math is often re-taught in English, and the supplemental math program is in English 0:27:28–0:35:14.

    Evidence shown: the superintendent's own minute-by-minute breakdown of a CP teacher's daily schedule, compared with the Center for Applied Linguistics definition of dual language and its Guiding Principles rubric. Also the 2014 program plan, district demographic data, and teachers' reports that math is re-taught in English.

    DLI parents' comment: Yet the superintendent's own data show that the program, as currently practiced, works for Spanish proficiency. On the AAPPL Spanish proficiency test, CP students are close to the national average for DLI programs in 3rd grade, at or above it in 4th, and pull further ahead in 5th and 8th grade. The longer students stay in the program, the more proficient they become 0:46:33–0:47:55.

  2. Teacher workload and sustainability.
    • CP teachers teach two groups of students and hold twice the parent conferences.
    • They coordinate with a partner teacher and adapt Spanish materials themselves.
    • Only one CP teacher is certified to teach Spanish.
    • Spanish-certified teachers are very hard to find. In June the district nearly opened a non-DLI kindergarten class because it couldn't fill a position 1:23:37.

    Evidence shown: a survey of CP staff (27 responses, including every classroom teacher), with one written comment displayed. Also the superintendent's conversations with teachers over a year and a half, and a count of teachers' certifications. On hiring: one example, last spring's kindergarten vacancy, which had no qualified applicants until late June. A Board member added that New Jersey has closed many language-teacher programs.

    DLI parents' comment: Staff at other schools were not surveyed (the superintendent acknowledged this), so the survey can't show whether the workload at CP is heavier than elsewhere. On hiring: the proposal itself needs two new certified Spanish teachers. The presentation did not address how nearby West Windsor-Plainsboro staffs its 50/50 Spanish and Mandarin immersion program in grades K–5, which New Jersey named a 2025–2028 model program. West Windsor came up only in public comment.

  3. Math for students whose first language isn't English. At CP, most of these students are from low-income families. They are behind their peers at other schools in math, which teachers think may be because math is taught in Spanish 0:59:34.

    Evidence shown: state test (NJSLA) results for multilingual learners at each elementary school, 2024–2026 combined. In math, 1 of 34 results at CP was proficient (3%), against 50% at Johnson Park, 68% at Littlebrook and 59% at Riverside. Among low-income multilingual learners: 0 of 29 at CP, 21% of 14 at Johnson Park. English language arts showed the same pattern, less sharply (10% at CP vs 21–52%).

    DLI parents' comment: The gap is large, but these are test results, not children (a child tested in three grades counts three times), and CP's group is far more low-income (29 of 34, against fewer than 10 of 106 at Littlebrook and Riverside). The slides don't show whether teaching math in Spanish is the cause.

  4. Demographics. The program was meant to serve English learners, low-income students, Black students and students with disabilities. Over 10 years CP has become less representative of these groups. It is now the only majority-white elementary school, though it also has the largest Hispanic population 0:35:14.

    Evidence shown: district enrollment data by school, comparing about 10 years ago with today, against the goals stated in the 2014 program plan. The superintendent said the shift was unintended and "no one's fault".

  5. Attrition.
    • Each cohort starts with about 40–45 students and is down to about 25 by 9th grade, mostly because families move away.
    • Of the first cohort (now seniors), only 8 are still taking Spanish, partly because the high school runs out of advanced courses 0:49:11.
    • Families who move to Princeton after first grade can't join DLI 1:02:45–1:09:06.

    Evidence shown: cohort counts from the district's student records (PowerSchool) at grades 1, 5, 8 and 9, plus a hand check of current high schoolers' schedules. The superintendent said most families left because they moved.

    DLI parents' comment: Reasons for leaving were not tracked, so the data can't show how many families left because of the program.

  6. Logistics and cost (the superintendent said these were not the main reason). About 160 students are bused out of the CP zone and about 65 in, at about $90k per bus route. The district has added three bus routes in three years for students not attending their neighborhood school.

    Evidence shown: five-year budget trends from district documents (transportation costs up 82% since 2021–22), a NJ Department of Education transportation-efficiency rating, and busing counts and per-route cost from about six weeks before the meeting.

    DLI parents' comment: No estimate was given of what the proposal would save.

Some of the superintendent's own findings were positive for the program

  • Parents and teachers love CP 0:41:38.
  • On state tests (spring 2026, students without special education services), CP is in the same range as the other schools in English: 69–79% proficient across grades 3–5, against 67–93% elsewhere. In math it is mixed: CP was second-best in 4th grade (86%) but lowest in 3rd and 5th (67% in 5th grade, against 86–89%). "No smoking gun" 0:56:57.

4. What parents, students and community members said

Public comment starts at 1:57:36. 21 people spoke: 15 adults (mostly CP parents, plus a CP alum and a Princeton University professor) and 6 current or former DLI students.

Where they stood

  • Everyone supported DLI itself. Many thanked the superintendent for a thorough review, and some were relieved the program isn't being cut entirely.
  • No one endorsed the plan as presented. 10 adults opposed it; 4 were open to parts of it but asked for more Spanish or for changes; 1 spoke only about keeping CP inclusive.
  • All 6 students spoke for DLI. Several said they had come expecting the program to be shut down.

Main objections (click to hear an example of each)

  1. It ends immersion; "a phase-out under another name." Without math and science in Spanish, Spanish becomes a subject "in a silo" rather than a language students learn in. Several speakers called it a "slippery slope" toward ending the program. 2:38:35 · 2:41:17
  2. It cuts Spanish time by more than half. Today's roughly 90–120 minutes a day falls to 45. One parent quoted the superintendent's own words back: becoming bilingual takes "one to two decades". 2:42:37
  3. It isn't enough to become proficient. Prof. Casey Lew-Williams, Princeton's psychology chair, who studies bilingual development, opposed the plan. 45 minutes is about 5–6% of a child's waking hours; the professor said they usually advise families to aim for 20–25% exposure, and at least 15%. 2:18:35

    Those percentages come from studies of young children at home, not school programs, but the research does back the point that less exposure means less proficiency.

  4. It doesn't fix the problems the data showed. The presentation identified two real issues: teacher workload, and math for English learners. The plan addresses workload but not math, "while throwing away" what works. 2:48:17
  5. It's too fast. CP is mid-construction, and the building will look different in two years. Several speakers asked the Board not to vote in November and to take time to explore other options. 2:14:07 · 2:39:18
  6. It removes the opt-in. Letting families from anywhere in Princeton choose CP is a matter of fairness. Giving one school 45 min/day while the others get 60 min/week, with no way in, will "foster more resentment". If intensive Spanish is good, offer it at every school. 2:15:25 · 2:37:14
  7. Other districts have found ways to make it work: West Windsor-Plainsboro next door, Davis, CA, and Allentown, PA. 2:00:10
  8. Transparency. Share the underlying data, and answer the pending public-records (OPRA) requests 2:02:32. One parent said a curriculum improvement proposed by teachers was turned down because of cost, and asked why 2:38:35.

4. Qué dijeron familias, estudiantes y comunidad

Los comentarios del público empiezan en 1:57:36. Hablaron 21 personas: 15 adultos (sobre todo familias de CP, además de una persona exalumna de CP y un miembro del profesorado de la Universidad de Princeton) y 6 estudiantes o exestudiantes de DLI.

Sus posiciones

  • Todos apoyaron DLI en sí. Muchos agradecieron al superintendente un análisis detallado, y algunos se sintieron aliviados de que el programa no se elimine por completo.
  • Nadie apoyó el plan tal como se presentó. 10 adultos se opusieron; 4 aceptaron partes pero pidieron más español o cambios; 1 habló solo de mantener CP como una escuela inclusiva.
  • Los 6 estudiantes hablaron a favor de DLI. Varios dijeron que habían venido pensando que iban a cerrar el programa.

Objeciones principales (haga clic para escuchar un ejemplo de cada una)

  1. Pone fin a la inmersión; "una eliminación con otro nombre". Sin matemáticas ni ciencias en español, el español pasa a ser una materia "aislada" en lugar de un idioma en el que se aprende. Varias personas lo describieron como el comienzo del fin del programa. 2:38:35 · 2:41:17
  2. Reduce el tiempo en español a menos de la mitad. Los 90–120 minutos diarios de hoy pasarían a 45. Una familia le recordó al superintendente sus propias palabras: hacerse bilingüe lleva "una o dos décadas". 2:42:37
  3. No basta para alcanzar dominio del idioma. Casey Lew-Williams, quien dirige el Departamento de Psicología de Princeton y estudia el desarrollo bilingüe, se opuso al plan. 45 minutos son un 5–6% de las horas que un niño pasa despierto; suele recomendar a las familias un 20–25% de exposición, y como mínimo un 15%. 2:18:35

    Esos porcentajes provienen de estudios de niños pequeños en casa, no de programas escolares, pero la investigación sí respalda que menos exposición significa menos dominio.

  4. No resuelve los problemas que mostraron los datos. La presentación identificó dos problemas reales: la carga docente y las matemáticas de los estudiantes que aprenden inglés. El plan atiende la carga docente pero no las matemáticas, "tirando por la borda" lo que funciona. 2:48:17
  5. Es demasiado rápido. CP está en obras y el edificio será distinto en dos años. Varias personas pidieron a la Junta no votar en noviembre y tomarse tiempo para estudiar otras opciones. 2:14:07 · 2:39:18
  6. Elimina la opción de inscribirse. Que familias de toda la ciudad puedan elegir CP es una cuestión de equidad. Dar a una escuela 45 min/día mientras las demás reciben 60 min/semana, sin forma de acceder, "generará más resentimiento". Si el español intensivo es bueno, que se ofrezca en todas las escuelas. 2:15:25 · 2:37:14
  7. Otros distritos han encontrado la forma de lograrlo: West Windsor-Plainsboro, aquí al lado; Davis (California); y Allentown (Pensilvania). 2:00:10
  8. Transparencia. Compartir los datos completos y responder a las solicitudes de registros públicos (OPRA) pendientes 2:02:32. Una familia dijo que se rechazó por costo una mejora curricular propuesta por los docentes, y preguntó por qué 2:38:35.
