What happened at the Oct 27 Board meeting

← How you can help

For parents who couldn't attend. Each timestamp links to that moment in the meeting video, so you can hear it yourself.

In short

  • Superintendent Michael LaSusa recommended ending dual language immersion at CP and replacing it with a "Spanish-intensive" program: one 45-minute Spanish class every day, with math and science taught in English.
  • CP would also become a neighborhood-only school, with no opting in from other parts of town.
  • Nothing has been decided yet. The Board could vote at its next meeting, if the superintendent formally recommends the plan. He wants a decision by Thanksgiving, before kindergarten registration. Watch the agenda when it is posted.
  • Email reaches the whole Board at ppsboe@princetonk12.org and the superintendent at michaellasusa@princetonk12.org. This page writes the email for you →

2. Reasons given for the recommendation

The full presentation starts at 0:10:41 and runs about 75 minutes. The main reasons given:

Note: the data behind these reasons has not been shared with parents, despite pending public-records (OPRA) requests, so families have not been able to check it themselves. Our comments below rely only on the analysis Dr. LaSusa presented at the meeting.

  1. The program as currently implemented falls short of the "Dual Language Immersion" promise.
    • CP is a "one-way" program (mostly English speakers learning Spanish). It has never had enough Spanish-dominant students to be "two-way".
    • By the superintendent's count, actual Spanish time falls short of half even in core subjects. Math is often re-taught in English, and the supplemental math program is in English 0:27:28–0:35:14.

    Evidence shown: the superintendent's own minute-by-minute breakdown of a CP teacher's daily schedule, compared with the Center for Applied Linguistics definition of dual language and its Guiding Principles rubric. Also the 2014 program plan, district demographic data, and teachers' reports that math is re-taught in English.

    DLI parents' comment: Yet the superintendent's own data show that the program, as currently implemented, works for Spanish proficiency. On the AAPPL Spanish proficiency test, CP students are close to the national average for DLI programs in 3rd grade, at or above it in 4th, and pull further ahead in 5th and 8th grade. The longer students stay in the program, the more proficient they become 0:46:33–0:47:55.

  2. Concerns about students' math performance. Parents raised math as a concern in the survey, and some teachers raised it with Dr. LaSusa directly. Comparing CP students without special education services with those at Johnson Park, Littlebrook and Riverside, he found only "a slight gap." Some teachers think learning math in Spanish is harder for children who are still learning English 0:59:34, so Dr. LaSusa looked at those students separately. Among those who are also from low-income families, fewer CP students met the state math standard than at the other schools.
    Evidence shown:
    • Most students (no special education services). On the 2026 state math test, CP students scored close to students at the other schools. CP was second-best in 4th grade and lowest in 3rd and 5th. The superintendent called it "a slight gap" 0:58:17.
    • Students still learning English who are also from low-income families. With grades 3–5 and the years 2024–2026 combined, 0 of 29 at CP met the state standard in math. At Johnson Park it was 3 of 14 (21%). At Littlebrook and Riverside combined it was 44%, but the slide says only "fewer than 10" for the number of students.
    DLI parents' comment: For most CP students, math is not a concern, and the superintendent said so himself. We would still like access to the data to understand it in more detail, but we accept his finding. The concern is about a small group of students who are still learning English and come from low-income families. A few notes:
    1. The fair comparison is with similar students. At CP, almost all students still learning English (29 of 34) come from low-income families. At Littlebrook and Riverside, fewer than 10 of 106 do. Children who are new to English and from low-income families face extra challenges at any school. So the fair comparison is between students who are still learning English and also from low-income families. Littlebrook and Riverside have too few of them to compare, which leaves Johnson Park.
    2. The groups are very small. Even with three grades and three years combined, the math comparison rests on 29 results at CP and 14 at Johnson Park. A few children can move the percentages a lot, which makes it hard to draw conclusions about the program.
    3. It isn't clear what is being counted. The slide doesn't say whether each number is a different student, or whether a student tested in more than one year is counted more than once. With numbers this small, knowing exactly how the data was put together matters.
    4. The math gap comes down to three results. Against similar students at Johnson Park, the difference in students who met the state standard is 0 of 29 at CP vs 3 of 14 at Johnson Park.
    5. In English, the result reverses. By the same analysis, CP comes out ahead in English: among the same students, 3 of 25 (12%) met the English standard at CP, against 1 of 13 (8%) at Johnson Park.
    6. There's no view of these students over time. These are one-time test results. Nothing showed how these students' results change from year to year, or how they do later in middle school.

    If these students need more help in math, the answer is to give them that help. That doesn't require ending immersion for every child.

  3. Teacher workload and sustainability. He said this is where he "put the most weight," based on a year and a half of conversations with CP teachers, whom he called the most professional he has worked with 0:16:25. He made four points:
    • CP teachers carry a double load. They teach two groups of students, hold twice the parent conferences, and must coordinate constantly with a partner teacher, which teachers called "demanding and draining" 0:20:01. Giving every class a single teacher for both languages isn't possible, because not all CP teachers are bilingual. Among homeroom teachers, 6 teach in English, 6 in Spanish, and 2 do both 0:21:12.
    • The program has lacked stability and support. Staff described constant year-to-year adjustments, and said the central office ("Valley Road") hadn't been engaged enough until recently 0:22:48.
    • Teachers are teaching Spanish without Spanish-teaching training. Only one CP teacher is certified to teach Spanish. The Spanish partner teachers teach Spanish language arts and make or adapt their own materials, which he called "a little bit unfair" to them 1:37:21.
    • Certified Spanish teachers are very hard to hire. Last spring a kindergarten opening had zero qualified applicants from March until the last week of June, even with an offer of over $100,000 1:23:37.

    Evidence shown: a survey of CP staff (27 responses, including every classroom teacher), with one written comment displayed in which a teacher questions how much longer the program can continue 0:44:17–0:46:33. Also a count of teachers' certifications and the one hiring example. A Board member added that New Jersey has closed many language-teacher programs.

    DLI parents' comment:
    1. Teachers love CP. In the same survey, every teacher said CP is caring and welcoming, and almost none want to transfer 0:44:17. The problem is the conditions they work under, not the school.
    2. Most of these problems come from how the program has been supported. He described instability, little central-office involvement, no structured Spanish curriculum, and teachers making their own materials.
    3. One change is already underway. This year's kindergarten was split into three classes instead of two to ease class sizes and teachers' load 0:21:12. It's too early to know how much that helps.
    4. His proposal puts new resources into CP that could fix DLI instead of ending it: curriculum development, a coordinator for CP, and certified Spanish teachers 1:24:52.
    5. His proposal faces the same hiring problem. It requires two new certified Spanish teachers 1:22:16.
    6. Nearby West Windsor-Plainsboro manages it. It staffs a 50/50 Spanish and Mandarin immersion program in grades K–5, which New Jersey named a 2025–2028 model program. The presentation didn't explain why West Windsor can do this and Princeton can't.
  4. Attrition. He raised it as a caveat to the strong Spanish proficiency results 0:47:55, and made two points:
    • Each class shrinks from about 41–45 in 1st grade to about 25–32 in high school. Most of the loss comes between 1st and 5th grade. He said families mostly leave because they move away; others switch schools or decide the program isn't the right fit. New students can't join after 1st grade, so they aren't replaced 0:49:11.
    • Students stop taking Spanish in high school. He highlighted that only 8 of 25 seniors and 14 of 25 juniors are in a Spanish class now. The main reason he gave is that after AP Spanish, students "run out of runway," because the high school has only one more advanced course. He proposes adding courses 0:50:30–0:51:48.

    Evidence shown: class-by-class counts for today's 9th–12th graders at grades 1, 5 and 8 and now, plus the Spanish course each high schooler is taking (slides 77–80). The current 8th grade was left out. It started 1st grade in 2019–20, when the pandemic hit, and he said it lost the most students.

    DLI parents' comment:
    1. Most students who reach high school keep taking Spanish through the most advanced levels. Of the 25 seniors, 14 took Spanish through AP or Spanish VI A, and 6 of those finished early. Among juniors, 17 of 25 are taking or have finished advanced Spanish. Among sophomores and freshmen, 24 of 25 and 31 of 32 are still in Spanish.
    2. We welcome more advanced courses at the high school. It would let DLI students keep studying Spanish right through graduation.
    3. The real issue is the drop in elementary school. Three of the four classes lost 10–12 students between 1st and 5th grade, and those seats can't be filled. There's a fix the district already uses: in middle school, new students who test in can join the DLI Spanish courses 0:50:30. Doing the same at CP would let children who already speak Spanish fill open seats.
    4. The Spanish-intensive plan is not a solution, as it would face the same late-arrival problem. When asked how a child arriving in 4th grade with no Spanish would fit into a daily Spanish class, the superintendent said it was still being worked out 1:40:17.
  5. Demographics. The program was meant to serve English learners, low-income students, Black students and students with disabilities. Over 10 years CP has become less representative of these groups. It is now the only majority-white elementary school, though it also has the largest Hispanic population 0:35:14.

    Evidence shown: district enrollment data by school, comparing about 10 years ago with today, against the goals stated in the 2014 program plan. The superintendent said the shift was unintended and "no one's fault".

    DLI parents' comment: Every Princeton family can choose to enroll in DLI at CP or opt out to another school, so CP's makeup reflects those choices. Indeed, the superintendent called the shift "no one's fault", and it's unclear why it would be a reason to end the program. The proposed Spanish-intensive alternative would also make CP a neighborhood school with no opt-in or opt-out 1:18:22. That removes the choice for every family, including the English learners and low-income families who now choose not to enroll in DLI and may also prefer a school without intensive Spanish.

  6. Transportation costs (the superintendent said this was not the main reason). He began by calling it a "false narrative" that the presentation would be about transportation costs, and said none of the teachers' concerns were about money 1:02:45. He still made three points:
    • Transportation is the fastest-growing cost. The district's budget grew about 21% over five years, roughly in line with inflation, but transportation grew 82%. Unlike health insurance, the district can't pass that increase on to taxpayers, so it takes money from other areas, including regular instruction. "Transportation is killing us" 1:04:04.
    • Princeton's busing is the least efficient in Mercer County, according to the state's rating. He said the main reason is that all Princeton schools start at the same time, so buses can't do one school run and then another. Changing start times would be "a different discussion" 1:05:20.
    • CP's opt-in/opt-out adds busing. About 159 students who live in the CP zone are bused to other schools, and about 65 are bused in to CP. The district has added three routes in three years for students not attending their neighborhood school, at about $90,000 per route, and he expects that cost to rise with diesel prices 1:06:35.

    Evidence shown: five-year budget trends from district documents; the state's transportation-efficiency ratings for Mercer County (Princeton 0.881, the others 1.26–1.78); school start times; and busing counts and the per-route cost from about six weeks before the meeting.

    DLI parents' comment:
    1. By his own account, the main cause is elsewhere. He named shared start times as the main reason Princeton's busing is inefficient. Ending DLI doesn't change that.
    2. No savings estimate was given. We weren't told how many routes the proposal would cut, or how much it would save each year.
    3. Savings would come slowly. The neighborhood-school change would start with incoming kindergartners 1:43:32, so older students' busing would continue for years.
    4. Not all of this busing is because of DLI. He said some students are bused out of CP because there isn't enough space yet, which construction will fix 1:18:22. The three added routes cover all students not at their neighborhood school, not only CP's.
    5. The trade-off wasn't weighed. If cost is a factor, families and the Board need the actual savings figure to weigh against what CP students would lose.

4. What parents, students and community members said

Public comment starts at 1:57:36. 21 people spoke: 15 adults (mostly CP parents, plus a CP alum and a Princeton University professor) and 6 current or former DLI students.

Where they stood

  • Everyone supported DLI itself. Many thanked the superintendent for a thorough review, and some were relieved the program isn't being cut entirely.
  • No one endorsed the plan as presented. 10 adults opposed it; 4 were open to parts of it but asked for more Spanish or for changes; 1 spoke only about keeping CP inclusive.
  • All 6 students spoke for DLI. Several said they had come expecting the program to be shut down.

Main objections (click to hear an example of each)

  1. It ends immersion; "a phase-out under another name." Without math and science in Spanish, Spanish becomes a subject "in a silo" rather than a language students learn in. Several speakers called it a "slippery slope" toward ending the program. 2:38:35 · 2:41:17
  2. It cuts Spanish time by more than half. Today's roughly 90–120 minutes a day falls to 45. One parent quoted the superintendent's own words back: becoming bilingual takes "one to two decades". 2:42:37
  3. It isn't enough to become proficient. Prof. Casey Lew-Williams, Princeton's psychology chair, who studies bilingual development, opposed the plan. 45 minutes is about 5–6% of a child's waking hours; the professor said they usually advise families to aim for 20–25% exposure, and at least 15%. 2:18:35

    Those percentages come from studies of young children at home, not school programs, but the research does back the point that less exposure means less proficiency.

  4. It doesn't fix the problems the data showed. The presentation identified two real issues: teacher workload, and math for English learners. The plan addresses workload but not math, "while throwing away" what works. 2:48:17
  5. It's too fast. CP is mid-construction, and the building will look different in two years. Several speakers asked the Board not to vote in November and to take time to explore other options. 2:14:07 · 2:39:18
  6. It removes the opt-in. Letting families from anywhere in Princeton choose CP is a matter of fairness. Giving one school 45 min/day while the others get 60 min/week, with no way in, will "foster more resentment". If intensive Spanish is good, offer it at every school. 2:15:25 · 2:37:14
  7. Other districts have found ways to make it work: West Windsor-Plainsboro next door, Davis, CA, and Allentown, PA. 2:00:10
  8. Transparency. Share the underlying data, and answer the pending public-records (OPRA) requests 2:02:32. One parent said a curriculum improvement proposed by teachers was turned down because of cost, and asked why 2:38:35.
